Third-party keyboards are useful. They can add features your phone's built-in keyboard doesn't have, like other languages, GIFs, translation or, in KEYCON's case, your sales catalog. But installing one is a decision about trust, and both Apple and Google treat it that way. Understanding the basics helps you make that decision with confidence.

What a keyboard can technically see

By design, a keyboard receives the keys you press while you're using it. That's how it shows your letters, corrects typos and suggests the next word. So the honest answer to “can a keyboard see what I type?” is: yes, while you type with it. The real questions are different:

  • Does it process your typing on the phone, or send it to a server?
  • Does it store what you type, and for how long?
  • Does it share anything with third parties, such as advertisers?

A trustworthy keyboard answers these questions clearly in its privacy policy, in language you can understand.

On iPhone: what “Allow Full Access” means

On iPhone, you add a keyboard in Settings › General › Keyboard › Keyboards › Add New Keyboard. By default, third-party keyboards run in a restricted mode: they can't access the internet and can't share data with their own app.

Some features need more than that, so iOS offers a switch called Allow Full Access. When you turn it on, Apple shows a warning that full access allows the developer to transmit anything you type. That warning is shown for every keyboard that asks for full access, whether it sends your typing anywhere or not. It describes what the keyboard could do, not what it does.

Two protections apply no matter what:

  • In password fields and other secure text fields, iOS automatically switches to its own keyboard.
  • Apps can choose not to allow third-party keyboards at all, which some banking apps do.
Why would a keyboard need Full Access? Usually to read data from its own app (for example, your saved replies or product catalog), to use the internet for features like downloading images, or to copy photos to the clipboard. The question to ask is whether the developer explains exactly what it's used for.

On Android: the input method warning

On Android, you enable a keyboard in your phone's settings, usually under System › Languages & input › On-screen keyboard (the exact path varies by manufacturer). When you turn on a new keyboard, Android warns that the input method may be able to collect all the text you type, including personal data such as passwords and credit card numbers.

Like Apple's warning, this is a general notice shown for every third-party keyboard. It's a reminder to install keyboards only from developers you trust, and to read how they handle your data.

A checklist for choosing a keyboard you can trust

  1. Who makes it? Install from the official App Store or Google Play listing, and check that the developer has a real website and contact information.
  2. Is typing processed on the device? The privacy policy should say plainly whether your keystrokes leave the phone.
  3. What does go to the server? Account details and files you upload on purpose are normal. Your typing history shouldn't be.
  4. Is your data sold or used for ads? Look for a clear statement.
  5. Why does it need each permission? Full Access on iPhone, or internet and storage on Android, should come with an explanation.
  6. Can you delete your account and data? A trustworthy app lets you do it from inside the app, without sending a letter.
  7. Is it updated? Regular updates are a good sign that someone is maintaining its security.

The bottom line

Every keyboard sees what you type while you type with it. That's not a flaw, it's how keyboards work. What separates a keyboard you can trust from one you can't is what happens next: whether your typing stays on the phone, what is sent to a server and why, and whether the company explains it clearly. Ask those questions, and you'll be able to enjoy a better keyboard without giving up your privacy.
